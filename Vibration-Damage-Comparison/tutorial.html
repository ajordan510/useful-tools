FIRST-TIME GUIDE · ABOUT 10 MINUTES

Follow one resonance from input to decision

A sine dwell and a random PSD describe different kinds of excitation. To compare them, ask what each one does to the same possible resonance, then account for the response amplitude and the number of cycles.

We will work through a 100 Hz oscillator, check its fatigue coverage and peak response, then ask what changes when the resonance frequency is unknown. The plots and numerical steps below are calculated by this tool from its checked reference case.

2 / LET THE HARDWARE FILTER THE INPUT

A resonance magnifies some frequencies more than others

An SDOF oscillator is a simple mass, spring and damper. The base motion is y(t); the mass moves by x(t). Their difference, z(t) = x(t) − y(t), represents deformation and serves as this tool’s fatigue stress proxy.

At excitation frequency f = fₙ, the displacement amplitude per peak g is g₀Q/(2πfₙ)². A higher Q generally means a sharper, larger resonant response. It affects both specifications; it does not simply multiply the fatigue coverage ratio by Q.

Build intuition: twice as many cycles gives twice the index. Twice the response amplitude gives 2⁴ = 16 times the index when b = 4. This is why response amplitude and the fatigue exponent deserve careful attention.

4 / FILTER THE ENTIRE RANDOM PSD

Random response contains a distribution of amplitudes

The oscillator responds to the whole 90–110 Hz PSD, not only its value at exactly 100 Hz. At each excitation frequency, the input PSD is multiplied by the squared relative-displacement transfer function.

Sz(f) = Sa(f) × |H(f, fₙ, Q)|²

RMS tells us the response’s spread, but fatigue depends strongly on its larger cycles. Dirlik uses spectral moments—integrals that describe the response’s intensity and frequency content—to estimate fatigue over the specified exposure time. It assumes a stationary Gaussian random response.

7 / CHANGE ONE THING AND PREDICT THE RESULT

Try the time-versus-amplitude distinction

This local experiment stays at the 100 Hz resonance with the same Q, b and input amplitudes. Predict what happens when the dwell cycles or random duration double, then change the controls.

Doubling dwell cycles doubles sine damage. Doubling random duration doubles expected random damage. Doubling both preserves their ratio. Neither change alters this steady-state sine amplitude or the selected 4σ random screen; a duration-based extreme-value model would be a different calculation.

8 / TRANSFER THE PROCESS TO YOUR OWN CASE

Use the same chain of reasoning

  1. Define the comparison. Use one axis and hardware configuration. Record the evidence and assumptions, then select a justified Q, fatigue exponent and damage target.
  2. Enter the sine specification with its actual units. Peak, RMS, peak-to-peak and double-amplitude values are different conventions. Credit sweeps only when they belong in the exposure; set traversals to 0 to exclude them.
  3. Choose the dwell question. Use a documented schedule for achieved exposure. Use independent assumed resonant dwells to screen a specification when mode locations are unknown. Enter caps and repeats; do not fabricate a combined schedule of hypothetical dwells.
  4. Enter the full random PSD and duration. Review breakpoints and level adjustments. Check the plotted inputs before interpreting the result.
  5. Choose the frequency scope and inspect the weakest points. Known modes assess only the listed frequencies; a full bank screens alternatives. Read fatigue coverage, peak response, the Q/b sensitivity matrix and warnings together.
  6. Keep an analysis record. Save the case JSON for editing, the CSV for numerical review and the HTML report for the assumptions and results. Refine near gaps before making a disposition.
A short glossary

fₙ: an oscillator’s natural frequency. SDOF: single degree of freedom. Q: the inverse of twice the damping ratio. b: fatigue exponent; larger b makes amplitude more influential. PSD: power spectral density, in g²/Hz here. R: heritage damage index divided by new random damage index. Coverage: R divided by the selected damage target.
