Comparing vibration exposures with fatigue and peak response spectra

A vibration specification describes motion at a test interface. Fatigue and peak response depend on how a structure responds to that motion. This workbench places the same family of linear oscillators under two exposures: a sine test and a random vibration requirement. It then compares accumulated fatigue severity and maximum response separately.

The result is a relative exposure comparison. It is useful for locating frequencies and assumptions that deserve attention. It is not an absolute life prediction. Throughout this chapter, “sine” is the credited exposure and “random” is the demand being compared against it. The physical meaning of conservatism depends on that direction.

1. Analysis sequence

Begin by identifying the hardware configuration, axis, specification revision, and credited exposure. Enter the sine profile in its original unit convention and the random specification as a one-sided acceleration power spectral density (PSD). Check the three input plots before studying any response result. A transcription error can produce a smooth and entirely misleading response curve.

Next choose natural frequencies, quality factors Q, fatigue exponents b, a random fatigue estimator, and a peak model. Run the comparison, examine the paired spectra and coverage curves, inspect the assumptions that govern, and refine the calculation where a decision depends on a narrow frequency interval. Record your interpretation separately from the calculated findings.

2. A common oscillator model

Let y(t) denote base displacement, x(t) absolute mass displacement, and z(t) = x(t) − y(t) relative displacement, all in metres. A linear single-degree-of-freedom oscillator obeys

z̈ + 2ζωnż + ωn²z = −ÿ
ωn = 2πfn,   Q = 1/(2ζ)

Here t is time in seconds, dots indicate time derivatives, fn is natural frequency in Hz, ωn is natural angular frequency in rad/s, and ζ is the dimensionless viscous damping ratio. Q is the dimensionless quality factor. These definitions refer to the linear viscous model; measured damping must be appropriate to the hardware and vibration level.

For a harmonic base acceleration at frequency f, the magnitude of the relative-displacement transfer function is

Hzg(f,fn,Q) = g0 / √[(ωn² − ω²)² + (ωnω/Q)²],   ω = 2πf

Hzg has units m/g, and g0 = 9.80665 m/s² per g. A sine input of ag peak g therefore produces peak relative displacement A = agHzg. At f = fn, Hzg = g0Q/ωn². Increasing Q increases resonant amplification and narrows its frequency region. The equation of motion is also developed in [1], Appendix B.

3. Input specifications and unit conventions

A sine curve may combine displacement, velocity and acceleration segments. The tool interpolates each segment as a power law in its entered quantity. For positive endpoints (f₁,u₁) and (f₂,u₂), it uses

u(f) = u₁(f/f₁)ln(u₂/u₁)/ln(f₂/f₁),   f₁ ≤ f ≤ f₂

u is the entered amplitude quantity and retains its specified units. The endpoint frequencies f₁ and f₂ are in Hz. “Double amplitude” means peak-to-peak, so displacement peak is half double amplitude. For harmonic motion, acceleration peak is ω² times displacement peak and ω times velocity peak. Peak is √2 times RMS only for a sinusoid.

DAin(f) = 2ag(f)g0 / [(2πf)² × 0.0254]

DAin is base-input displacement in inches peak-to-peak; 0.0254 is metres per inch. The displacement plot includes the sine multiplier, but not separate dwell acceleration overrides. Check those overrides in the exposure records.

The random input Ga(f) is a one-sided PSD in g²/Hz. Its integral is acceleration variance in g². Breakpoints are interpolated logarithmically in frequency and PSD. Input is zero outside the supplied frequency band; that is a modeling assumption, not evidence that the physical environment contains no energy there. A PSD change of d decibels multiplies Ga by 10d/10, where d is the entered dB adjustment.

The Excel template uses profile IDs to connect multiple sine and random spectra to comparison cases. Keep its header names and enter literal values rather than formulas. Settings are listed one per row to avoid a very wide input sheet. Unspecified settings inherit the documented illustrative defaults, which must be reviewed before analysis. Import preview and validation occur before the current study is replaced.

4. Fatigue accumulation and the meaning of the damage index

Consider a power-law relation Nf(S) = C/Sb. Nf is the cycles to failure at stress amplitude S, b is the positive fatigue exponent, and C is the intercept with units consistent with S. Under linear accumulation, a set of ni cycles at amplitude Si contributes ΣniSib/C.

If stress amplitude is proportional to relative-displacement amplitude by the same factor for both exposures, that conversion factor and C cancel in their ratio. The workbench therefore calculates a normalized severity index

D = Σ ni(Ai/zref)b,   zref = 0.001 m

D is dimensionless but is not an absolute Miner failure fraction. Ai is displacement cycle amplitude in metres, ni is the cycle count, and zref is a fixed normalization length. Cycle amplitude is half range. Using full range for only one exposure introduces an error of 2b.

Select b from the fatigue behavior of the relevant material, detail and operating conditions. A weld, solder joint, smooth specimen and notched component need not share an exponent. The illustrative b = 4, 6, 8 matrix is a sensitivity study, not a material-property recommendation. Higher b gives greater weight to large cycles but does not necessarily make a ratio more conservative. For spectral-method background and limits see [2] and [3].

5. Sine dwell and sweep damage

A documented dwell at excitation frequency fd produces amplitude Ad = adHzg(fd,fn,Q). Here ad is the scaled base acceleration in peak g. With an hour cap h, cycle cap Ccap, and repetition count r, credited exposure is

td = min(3600h, Ccap/fd) over enabled caps
nd = r fdtd
Dd = nd(Ad/zref)b

td is duration per repetition in seconds; nd is total cycles. A zero cap disables that cap; at least one cap must remain positive. Both caps describe the same dwell, so they are not added. Documented dwells at different excitation frequencies all contribute to each oscillator through its transfer function.

In independent assumed-resonance mode, set fd = fn separately for each oscillator. Use the local sine input level and the same exposure rule at that frequency. No other hypothetical dwell contributes. Each frequency receives the complete assumed allowance. Nothing is summed across natural frequencies, and refining the oscillator bank does not change the credited dwell at an existing frequency. Outside the sine profile, assumed dwell credit is zero.

For P one-way sweep traversals, the quasi-steady sweep contribution is

Dsw = P ∫ f [A(f)/zref]b (dt/df) df
dt/df = 60/[voct ln(2) f]   or   60/vlin

P is the traversal count; A(f) is steady harmonic response amplitude; voct is rate in octaves/minute; vlin is rate in Hz/minute. The integral spans credited sine segments. Total sine index is Ds = Dsw + ΣDd. Fast sweeps and short dwells may not reach the assumed steady response. Review the transient indicators before crediting their predicted exposure.

6. Random response and fatigue damage spectra

Filter the complete input PSD through each oscillator:

Gz(f) = |Hzg(f)|² Ga(f)
mj = ∫ fjGz(f) df,   j ∈ {0,1,2,4}
σ = √m₀,   ν₀ = √(m₂/m₀),   νp = √(m₄/m₂),   α₂ = m₂/√(m₀m₄)

Gz is displacement PSD in m²/Hz. Moment mj has units m²Hzj. σ is response RMS in metres for zero-mean response. ν₀ is expected positive-slope zero-crossing rate in Hz; νp is expected peak rate in Hz. α₂ is a dimensionless bandwidth indicator, approaching one for narrowband response. These moments use frequency in Hz. References that use angular-frequency moments require a 1/(2π) factor in crossing rates; adding that factor here would be incorrect.

In the narrowband Gaussian approximation, cycle amplitudes follow a Rayleigh distribution. Expected damage over total random duration T seconds is

DNB = Tν₀(√2σ/zref)bΓ(1 + b/2)

Γ is the gamma function, Γ(u) = ∫₀∞tu−1e−tdt for positive argument u; t in this integral is a dimensionless integration variable. Total T equals minutes per exposure × 60 × repetitions. The narrowband result is a useful limit and diagnostic, not a universal correction for broadband loading.

Dirlik replaces the single Rayleigh amplitude distribution with an empirical mixture inferred from spectral moments. Define dimensionless xm, d₁, d₂, d₃, qD and rD as follows. The subscript D distinguishes distribution parameters from modal Q and coverage R.

xm = (m₁/m₀)√(m₂/m₄)
d₁ = 2(xm − α₂²)/(1 + α₂²)
rD = (α₂ − xm − d₁²)/(1 − α₂ − d₁ + d₁²)
d₂ = (1 − α₂ − d₁ + d₁²)/(1 − rD),   d₃ = 1 − d₁ − d₂
qD = 1.25(α₂ − d₃ − d₂rD)/d₁
DD = Tνp(σ/zref)b{d₁qDbΓ(1+b) + 2b/2Γ(1+b/2)[d₂|rD|b + d₃]}

DD is the Dirlik expected index. The engine checks coefficient validity and uses the Rayleigh limit near α₂ = 1. This is an amplitude-form implementation of the model, with published implementation checks in [4]. The approximation assumes a zero-mean stationary Gaussian process. Strongly separated spectral bands merit particular review; accurate numerical integration does not remove model-form uncertainty.

7. Peak response and extreme-response estimates

Peak response is a separate screen. A long low-amplitude exposure can accumulate fatigue without reaching the largest peak of a short exposure. Conversely, matching one peak says little about the number of damaging cycles.

The sine peak Zs is the largest credited steady relative-displacement amplitude among documented dwells and enabled sweeps. In assumed mode only the oscillator's own hypothetical dwell and enabled sweeps are eligible. The random RMS response spectrum is σ(fn), often called a VRS. It is not a peak spectrum.

Zr,fixed = kσ

Zr,fixed is the selected random peak in metres and k is a user-selected dimensionless factor. Fixed k is independent of exposure duration. It has no assigned event non-exceedance probability without an additional statistical model.

The default workbench alternative is the approximate duration/probability model from [1], equation (4):

N = ν₀T,   L = √(2 ln N)
kext = (L + γE/L) √{−ln[1 − p1/N]/ln N}
Zr = kextσ,   γE = 0.5772

N is expected response cycles, L an intermediate dimensionless factor, γE rounded Euler constant, p the entered non-exceedance probability divided by 100, kext the frequency-dependent crest factor, and Zr the estimated maximum absolute random response in metres. ln denotes natural logarithm. p concerns one oscillator over total exposure; it is not simultaneous coverage of the oscillator bank.

The independent-Rayleigh URS expression in [5], section 12.5.3.2.4 is σ√{−2ln[1−p1/N]}. The implemented Irvine expression multiplies that estimate by 1 + γE/(2ln N). The correction and assumed cycle independence make the entered probability nominal, not an exact calibrated confidence statement. Response bandwidth and damping affect applicability. The workbench requires N ≥ 100 and flags α₂ < 0.9; these are review guards, not proven accuracy bounds.

A higher p raises the random demand and lowers sine-over-random peak coverage. That is a more demanding screen in this comparison direction. It should not be generalized to a reversed claim that random testing covers a sine or shock requirement. Repeated stationary exposures are combined through total T; startup transients and dependence between event histories are not modeled.

8. Reading coverage and scenario results

R = Ds/Dr,   CF = R/F
CP = Zs/Zr

Dr is the selected random damage estimate; F is the required dimensionless fatigue factor; CF is fatigue coverage; and CP is peak coverage. Each target is one. The paired fatigue source plot shows Ds and FDr, so dividing those curves gives CF directly. A logarithmic axis displays the original values with unequal linear spacing; the values themselves are not logarithms.

Each b × Q cell is a complete independent assessment. Compare normalized coverage across b, because raw normalized damage indices change meaning with the exponent. Peak response depends on Q and the peak assumptions, not b. The grouped columns show minima that may occur at different frequencies. Select a heatmap cell and inspect the continuous curves to locate those frequencies.

Known-mode scope assesses only the listed frequencies. Faint connecting curves are context. Full-bank scope assesses every sampled frequency. Zooming, panning, changing axis scales or hiding curves changes the view only. Saved plot views are used by exports. Downward markers on a logarithmic axis indicate true zero values plotted at the visual floor; CSV retains the zeros.

Reading the interactive summaries

The Selected b / Q controls choose a single oscillator-assumption pair for the source spectra. The Coverage display control switches the fatigue and peak coverage curves between that pair and a shaded range across all calculated pairs. At each natural frequency, the lower boundary is the smallest coverage across those pairs and the upper boundary is the largest. The selected pair remains visible as a solid blue curve. These boundaries are a sensitivity range, not a confidence interval or a sum of simultaneous resonances. Raw damage indices for different exponents are not combined.

The scenario heatmap and grouped columns can summarize the minimum over all assessed frequencies or show values at one calculated frequency. In overall mode, each cell may govern at a different frequency. In frequency-specific mode, all cells refer to the same oscillator frequency. The dropdown and slider use actual calculated points; no interpolation is applied. Only assessed points are offered, so a listed-modes assessment restricts the selector to those modes. If a saved frequency is no longer available after changing inputs, the nearest assessed point on a logarithmic frequency scale is selected and its actual value is displayed. The heatmap may show fatigue or peak coverage; peak values repeat across b because the peak model does not depend on the fatigue exponent.

Clicking a heatmap cell selects its b/Q pair and outlines it. Changing this display does not change the assessment scope, exposure, or headline minima. Saved studies and report figures retain these display choices; report takeaways remain overall minima. A frequency-specific plot is therefore a local diagnostic, not proof of coverage elsewhere.

9. Worked example at 100 Hz

Consider an illustrative oscillator with fn = 100 Hz, Q = 10 and b = 4. A 1 g peak dwell at 100 Hz lasts 30 s (3,000 cycles). Compare it with 0.03 g²/Hz from 90 to 110 Hz for 30 s. There are no sweeps, F = 1, and only the known 100 Hz mode is assessed. These inputs allow the calculation to be followed without claiming a material-specific fatigue life.

At resonance, A = g0Q/(2π × 100)² = 0.2484053 mm. Divide that amplitude by zref = 1 mm, raise it to b = 4, and multiply by 3,000 cycles. The sine index is 11.4226.

The fatigue ratio is slightly above one while the peak result may tell a different story. Neither result replaces the other. The numerical values above are generated from the same released engine during the application build. Then run it to explore the requirement, fatigue and peak plots with zoom and axis controls.

As an exercise, double the random duration. Expected random damage doubles, so fatigue coverage halves. The extreme response rises more slowly because its peak factor depends logarithmically on the cycle count. Next change to independent assumed dwell mode: each oscillator receives a resonant dwell of its own, instead of sharing the single documented 100 Hz dwell. Compare the surrounding frequencies to see why these answer different engineering questions.

10. Numerical accuracy and review

The engine integrates in frequency with Gaussian quadrature, adding subdivisions around resonance and PSD breakpoints. It separately samples the oscillator bank. These are two distinct numerical resolutions: refining integration improves each oscillator result, whereas refining bank density helps locate narrow minima between oscillators.

Each scenario checks refined integration at governing fatigue and peak frequencies. A change above 0.5% raises a review message. That threshold is a numerical screening criterion. It is not a physical model-error bound. Use Analysis → Double frequency density when a conclusion depends on a crossing or minimum, compare the resulting minimum and frequency, and retain sufficient margin against sampling uncertainty.

A reproducible simulation review used 32 Gaussian histories of 120 s for each of two spectra, with b = 4 and 6. Ensemble RMS differed by less than 0.3% and mean rainflow damage by 0.6–3.6% from the spectral calculation. These examples do not establish accuracy for arbitrary spectra. In separate 600-trial, 5 s peak experiments, nominal 50% non-exceedance corresponded to about 86% observed non-exceedance for the narrowband case and 57% for the broader case. The nominal 99% level was not resolved precisely by this trial count. This reinforces the approximate interpretation of the peak model, even above the 100-cycle guard.

Independent quadrature, published regressions and analytical scaling checks verify the numerical implementation. Synthesized Gaussian histories and rainflow comparisons exercise model behavior with finite-record variability. Their random outcomes require distributions and uncertainty, not exact equality to an expected spectral estimate. The verification record accompanies the source distribution.

11. Applicability and engineering evidence

The oscillator bank represents potential resonances, not a measured modal model. Cancellation of the stress conversion requires equivalent dynamics and stress mapping in both exposures. Changes in mounting, mass, preload, axis, configuration or temperature can defeat that assumption. The tool excludes nonlinearities, multiaxial interaction, mean-stress correction, endurance limits and consumed-life accounting.

PSD information does not uniquely determine a non-Gaussian or nonstationary history. If those features matter, use a validated history-based or stress-based model. A modal test or measured response can support Q selection and test the stress-proxy assumption. The measured test completion record determines what exposure can actually be credited.

The shortest useful assessment record includes source specifications and revisions, configuration and axis, duration and repetitions, dwell interpretation, scope, b/Q rationale, random estimator, peak model and probability/factor, convergence findings, governing frequencies, and analyst interpretation. The workbench preserves these inputs in study files and report notes.
